Generative models operate on continuous and discrete state spaces. This course develops their mathematical, algorithmic, and empirical foundations, with discrete state modeling as the central focus.

Course logistics: Tuesdays and Thursdays, 8:30–10:00 a.m. in Towne 337. Fall 2026.

Course structure

The semester moves through four connected units: mathematical foundations of generative transport; continuous generative models; discrete stochastic processes and generative frameworks; and optimal transport with stochastic control. Derivations are paired with coding workshops, empirical evaluation, and applications to DNA, RNA, peptides, proteins, language, code, and images.

Learning objectives

  1. Develop a rigorous understanding of probability, the simplex, continuous-time Markov chains, the master equation, and generative transport.
  2. Derive, analyze, and implement classical generative models, discrete diffusion, discrete flow matching, optimal transport, stochastic control, and discrete Schrödinger bridges.
  3. Apply these frameworks to biological sequences and other discrete data.
  4. Translate mathematical specifications into implementations and evaluate them with benchmarks and ablations.
  5. Read current research and design, communicate, and defend novel discrete generative modeling strategies.

Prerequisites

Students must have completed CIS 5190, CIS 5200, or ESE 5460.

Texts and communication

Original lecture notes by Professor Chatterjee cover the course. Deep Generative Modeling by Jakub M. Tomczak (Springer, 2024) is a suggested introductory reference.

Announcements and official course communication are distributed through Canvas. Use Ed Discussion for public course questions and email the teaching team for individual matters.

Grading

10%Participation
30%Two written exams
60%Three group projects

There is no curve. Final percentages use the traditional scale described in the official syllabus. Grades are posted in Canvas.

Participation

Participation includes contributions to lecture derivations, algorithm-design sessions, and coding workshops. Students should arrive on time and must arrive by 8:40 a.m. to be marked present. See the official syllabus for absence and documentation rules.

Exams

Two closed-book written exams, worth 15% each, emphasize mathematical reasoning, proofs, derivations, stochastic-process analysis, and algorithm design. One reference sheet is allowed.

Group projects

  • Project 1 — NeurIPS Workshop (15%): an empirical study focused on execution, benchmarking, ablation, and quantitative evaluation.
  • Project 2 — ICLR Workshop (20%): implementation and theoretical analysis of established discrete generative modeling frameworks.
  • Final Project — ICML Main Conference (25%): a novel method applied to a biological sequence design problem, with a complete code repository and ICML-style paper.

Every project concludes with a 30-minute group defense: a 10-minute presentation and 20 minutes of questions from the teaching team.

Practice problems

There are no graded problem sets. Ungraded practice problems with full solutions will be released throughout the semester.
